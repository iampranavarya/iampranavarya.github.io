A Touchscreen MacBook Pro for Editors: Useful Tool or Expensive Gimmick?

Apple's MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR brings a tandem OLED touchscreen to the Mac. What matters for colour grading and editing on location, and what does not.

Pranav AryaOctober 31, 2026 · 2 min read

On October 28, Apple announced the MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR display: its first touchscreen Mac. Preorders opened the same day and the first deliveries start on November 6. It comes in 14-inch and 16-inch sizes with M5 Pro or M5 Max chips, starting at $2,899 for the 14-inch model. Separately, Apple also refreshed the regular 14-inch MacBook Pro with the M6 chip and its familiar mini-LED screen.

The touch screen gets the headlines. For people who edit and grade on the road, the display itself is the bigger story.

The display is the real upgrade

PROLED XDR is a tandem OLED panel, two OLED layers stacked, the same approach Apple first used in the iPad Pro. Apple rates it at 1,000 nits sustained full-screen and 2,000 nits peak, with a 10,000,000:1 contrast ratio, 1 billion colours and coverage of both P3 and Adobe RGB. There is also a nano-texture option for bright rooms.

For grading on location, that combination matters:

  • True blacks. OLED does not have the blooming you sometimes see around bright objects on mini-LED, which makes judging shadows and HDR highlights easier.
  • Sustained brightness. Usable in a bright venue or a car, where most laptop screens wash out.
  • Wide gamut. P3 for video work and Adobe RGB for photographers delivering print.

None of this replaces a calibrated reference monitor for final HDR delivery. But for reviewing rushes on set and doing a solid first grade in a hotel room, it is a big step up.

Touch: helpful, with limits

Touch on a laptop is not new in the PC world, and the honest experience there is that you use it less than you expect. Where it can genuinely help editors:

  • Scrubbing and nudging on a timeline when the trackpad feels slow.
  • Showing a client a cut, letting them point and tap instead of describing timecodes.
  • Colour wheels and sliders, if the apps adopt touch properly.

That last point is the open question. Apple says dedicated touch updates for third-party apps, including DaVinci Resolve and Adobe Lightroom, are due later this year. Until those arrive, touch in pro apps will be limited. And yes, fingerprints on a screen you grade on is a real concern.

Which MacBook Pro I would choose

If you grade on location or deliver HDR, the PROLED model is the interesting one, and the M5 Max version makes sense for heavy 6K or RAW timelines. If you mostly edit in the studio on an external reference monitor, the M6 model with mini-LED gets you the newer chip for less money, and the touchscreen will not change your day.

As always with first-generation hardware, I would wait for real-world reviews on brightness consistency, battery life and how well touch works inside Resolve and Premiere before ordering a machine you depend on.
