Why I Started Carrying Binoculars to Every Outdoor Shoot

A filmmaker's case for keeping a compact pair of binoculars in the bag, and what to actually look for if you're buying your first pair.

Pranav AryaSeptember 10, 2025 · 2 min read

I picked up a pair of compact binoculars almost by accident before a motorsport shoot last year, mostly to scout marshal positions before a race started. I haven't shot an outdoor event without them since. It's a strange piece of gear to recommend on a filmmaking blog, but scouting a location with your eyes before you commit a lens to it changes how you shoot.

What compact binoculars are actually for

I'm not glassing for birds. I'm checking sightlines from a hundred meters out, figuring out which grandstand section catches clean light at 4pm, spotting where a marshal is going to stand and block my angle twenty minutes before it happens. A 300mm lens can do some of this, but it's heavy to hold up for ten minutes of scouting and it locks you into thinking about framing before you've decided where to stand.

For that job, an 8x32 or 10x32 compact pair is enough. You don't need the low-light performance of a full-size 42mm objective for daytime scouting, and the smaller body means it actually lives in the bag instead of getting left in the car because it's annoying to carry.

What to actually check before buying

Ignore magnification as the headline number. Eye relief matters more if you wear glasses, and field of view matters more than most first-time buyers expect, a narrow field makes scanning a crowd or a track slow and frustrating. Handle a pair before buying if you possibly can. The difference between a comfortable and uncomfortable eyecup only shows up after you've held them up for a few minutes, not in the first five seconds in a shop.

  • 8x32 or 10x32 for daytime scouting, skip 42mm unless you shoot dawn or dusk often
  • Check field of view in degrees, not just magnification
  • Waterproof and fog-proof ratings matter more outdoors than most people assume

The unexpected use case

The thing nobody tells you: binoculars are a faster way to check your own frame from a distance than walking to a monitor. I've used mine to check a drone's position against a skyline from the launch point more times than I've used them for actual scouting at this point. Cheap trick, works every time, and it costs nothing to try on your next shoot.
